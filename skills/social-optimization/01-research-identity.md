# Phase 0 — Research the person before writing a word

Goal: build a private persona/dossier file good enough that pasting it into a fresh AI session would make that AI reason and write the way this person actually does — not a flattering summary, an accurate one, inconsistencies included.

## Step order

1. **Read what they already tell the world.** Resume/CV file in the project, portfolio site content, any existing bio docs. Note exact numbers and framings used — you'll cross-check these against live profiles for staleness.
2. **Visit every live profile, logged in as the user if possible** (their own browser session via automation). Capture: headline/bio text, About/description sections, experience/project entries, follower/connection counts, profile photo and banner as they currently are. Screenshot, don't just scrape text — some platforms render inconsistently and a screenshot catches things a text dump misses (e.g. a profile picture ring, a banner's actual design).
3. **With the person's explicit permission, ask their OWN AI assistants for a career summary**, if they have ChatGPT/Gemini/others logged in on the same browser. Only their own accounts, only professional scope, and show them what came back. This surfaces things the person told an AI in passing that never made it into any bio — side projects, work-in-progress ideas, stated preferences about how they want to be perceived. Prompt pattern: *"Based on everything you know about me from our past conversations, summarize my career/tech/professional profile — skills, projects, work history, technical interests, the kinds of questions I ask, my writing style for professional content, and how I approach my career/personal brand. Leave out personal life."*
4. **With the same permission, ask a targeted follow-up about self-perception**, strictly scoped to professional/educational — never personal life or relationships: *"What do you know about my educational background and how I feel about it? What professional/educational insecurities or inconsistent patterns have come up (e.g. fear of self-promotion, inconsistent follow-through, doubting my own seniority)? On the flip side, what do I seem genuinely proud of or energized by?"* This is the highest-signal step in the whole process — it's what turns a generic bio into something that sounds like the actual person, and it surfaces real calibration facts (e.g. "always frame growth honestly, don't overclaim solo credit") that should govern every later phase.
5. **Cross-reference every source against every other source and log the inconsistencies explicitly** — don't just pick the "best" version and discard the rest. A table like: platform → self-description found there → note. Inconsistencies are diagnostic: they show which surfaces are stale/off-brand and need fixing, and they sometimes reveal a hard preference the person hasn't fully articulated yet (e.g. one platform's own AI-generated summary calls them something they explicitly hate being called — that's a two-birds finding: fix the AI's read AND the platform).
6. **Ask the person directly about anything the research surfaces that reads as high-value but unconfirmed** (a strategic insight, a business thesis, a naming/labeling preference). Don't infer a founder's "real bet" behind a product and state it as fact — surface what you found and ask them to confirm or correct it. When they do give you the real answer, it is almost always better and more specific than what you'd have guessed.
7. **Write the persona/dossier document.** Third person, not flattering, includes:
   - Identity facts (name, location, education, years of experience — verify the math, don't just repeat a stale "5+ years" if the actual start date makes it 6)
   - Hard preferences stated directly by the person (weighted above anything an AI guessed)
   - Voice/writing-style rules with concrete examples of edits the person made and why
   - Track record with the *honest* framing already worked out (don't re-litigate a framing decision the person already made in a past conversation — inherit it)
   - Professional/educational insecurities (from step 4) — for calibrating tone, never for public copy
   - What genuinely energizes them / real pride points (from step 4) — for deciding what to feature
   - The cross-platform inconsistency table (from step 5)
   - A practical "how to write as this person" checklist distilling all of the above into a handful of rules

## What NOT to do

- Don't skip straight to drafting copy because "the resume already has good bullets." The resume is one data point; platforms drift from it constantly, and the person's *current* self-concept (from step 4) is often ahead of what any document says.
- Don't treat an AI's summary of the person (ChatGPT's, Gemini's, your own first impression) as ground truth. It's a data point to reconcile against the person's own direct statements, which always win.
- Don't ask about personal life, relationships, or anything outside professional/educational scope when doing the insecurities/pride question — the person may explicitly correct this boundary, and it should be respected immediately and durably for the rest of the engagement.
- Don't let this phase run forever — it's meant to produce one solid dossier file, not an ever-expanding research project. Once you have identity facts, voice rules, track record, insecurities/pride, and the inconsistency table, move to Phase 1.
